# The conflict

Two design goals are both serious. They cannot be true of every lesson at once.

**Phase 1.** The instructional dossier says more technical depth destroys self-pacing when every lesson is a full distributed system. The core path keeps twenty decisions. Each core lesson teaches one: what arrives, which part decides, what it refuses, and what it writes. Code on that path is at most one completion inside one file, and only when a trace cannot show the behavior. Extension labs hold the subsystems. Skipping a lab leaves the next core lesson open and forbids a capstone sentence that claims the lab was run. Prerequisites and exit criteria are set. Hours, days, and weeks are not. See `Idea_CrossCutting/06-instructional-design/README.md`, `01-learning-science.md`, and `03-proposed-progression.md`.

**The redesign brief.** Principal-level forward-deployed work is three productions: investigate a messy enterprise, implement locally, and operate on AWS. A decision memo with no running boundary, and no operational check, is below that job. A local toy with no cloud control is also below it.

The dossier already separates the decision bundle from the implementation bundle, and it warns that a large gate repeated twenty times is the completion failure. The brief raises the standard of the decision bundle until it includes a local system and a cloud system. That is the conflict.

## Side A: one decision, one completion, no clock

The Government Digital Service tells a team not to build the service while it is still finding out whether the service should exist. The discovery guidance, last updated 21 June 2021, says: "You should not start building your service in discovery." Discovery ends when the team decides whether to go on to alpha. Stopping is a successful end: "It's not a failure to stop at the end of the discovery phase if your research shows that's the best thing to do." The work includes reframing a handed-down solution as a problem, agreeing what is outside the problem, and quantifying what the problem currently costs. There is "no set time period," and "around 4 to 8 weeks is typical," with the purpose setting the length. Those weeks describe a multidisciplinary team on a real service. They are not a lesson length. The usable part is the definition of done: a decision, including a decision to stop, grounded in users, constraints, and cost.

Alpha, last updated 8 May 2019, is a prototype phase with a narrow job. "You do not have to prototype the user’s entire wider journey." The team does "the minimum you need to test your riskiest assumptions." Alpha code is "just complex enough to let you test different ideas, not production quality code," and the team should "expect to throw away any code." Alpha is not public. The typical "6 to 8 weeks" is again a team phase, not a chapter quota. The usable part is the same grain Phase 1 chose: one risky assumption, one disposable proof, then a decision about what is worth taking forward.

Bloom's *Learning for Mastery* (1968), opened as the ERIC full text ED053419, treats aptitude as time: "aptitude is the amount of time required by the learner to attain mastery of a learning task." He reports, from the International Study of Educational Achievement, that once the extreme 5 percent are omitted, some 13-year-olds spend about six times as long on mathematics homework as others. He thinks better instruction might compress that ratio toward three to one. He also writes that a learner who is frustrated "must (in self-defense) reduce the amount of time he devotes to learning," and that "all students must sooner or later give up a task if it is too painful for them." Perseverance is not a virtue to spend. "There seems to be little reason to make learning so difficult that only a small proportion of the students can persevere to mastery."

This course has no tutor sitting with each reader. Bloom's own picture of wide mastery assumes instruction that can vary and time that can vary. A mixed group of employed government staff, some of whom program and some of whom do not, working alone, does not have either variable in unlimited supply. A painful gate is abandoned. A gate that is a distributed system is painful for anyone who has not already chunked that system. Phase 1's rule, one trap and one decision, with a second subsystem moved to a lab, is the mastery strategy available here: small enough to revise, specific enough to fail.

Oregon State University's Center for Teaching and Learning, opened for this note, describes specifications grading in Nilson's form. Assignments are satisfactory or unsatisfactory, with no partial credit. A higher course standing is a bundle of more work, or of more advanced outcomes, rather than a percentage of a single large assignment. Students may revise. The page also repeats Nilson's token cap, on the order of two to five tokens, because unlimited revision costs instructor time. This course has automated checks and no instructor quota, so a token cap on the only path would recreate a calendar by another name. The part that transfers is the bundle. Core work is one specification, met or not yet. Distinction is another bundle. It is not half-credit for a half-built platform.

Side A therefore says: twenty decisions can stay, each one small; code stays at one completion in one file when a trace cannot show the refusal; AWS, fusion, failover, surge, and adapters are labs; the capstone may not claim a lab that did not run. A timetable is the wrong response to a time problem. The right response is a smaller specification.

## Side B: investigate, implement, operate

The same service manual refuses to let a discovery memo stand in for a service that people depend on.

Point 14 of the Service Standard, "Operate a reliable service," was updated on 29 January 2026. Teams must test "in an environment that’s as similar to live as possible," monitor, and keep "a proportionate, sustainable plan to respond to problems." They must "monitor outcomes for users and ethical issues such as bias, not just technical faults." Quality assurance "must be overseen by the service team, not left to automated tools." A chapter that ends in a refusal paragraph has not met point 14. A linter has not met it either. The January 2026 sentence is explicit that an automated check is not the whole of assurance.

Point 10, opened with point 14, says the team identifies metrics that show whether the service is solving the problem, and uses that data to decide how to fix it. Point 11 says a technology choice includes build versus buy, the cost of ownership, legacy systems the service depends on, and an understanding of how the tools work, including artificial intelligence. Investigation that never touches a legacy interface, and an architecture slide that never names a metric, miss those points.

Zinkevich's *Rules of Machine Learning*, opened on Google's developer site, is aimed at people who already know some machine learning and are about to build a product. The overview says to make the pipeline solid end to end, start with a reasonable objective, add simple features, and keep the pipeline solid. "Adding complexity slows future releases." Rule 1 says to launch without machine learning when a heuristic will do, because machine learning needs data. Rule 2 says to design and implement metrics before formalizing the model, so the team is not later "grepping for strings in logs." Rule 4 says the first model stays simple and the infrastructure has to be right: examples reach the learner, "good" and "bad" are defined, and the model is integrated into the application. Rule 5 says to test that infrastructure independently of the learning. Phase II, feature engineering and richer models, begins after an end-to-end system already has tests.

Those rules cut against a custom model in every lesson. They also cut against a course whose only artifact is a memo. "Design and implement metrics" is an implementation sentence. A pipeline that is never run cannot be tested independently of the model. A principal-level engineer who can only describe the heuristic has not done Rule 2 or Rule 5.

The ML Test Score (Breck, Cai, Nielsen, Salib, and Sculley, IEEE Big Data 2017) is the production rubric that matches those rules. The PDF opened for this note defines 28 tests in four sections: data, model, infrastructure, and monitoring. The paper is about production systems, in the authors' words mostly supervised models trained on a continuing basis and served at low latency. It says most of the recommendations still apply more widely. It also says the rubric runs from a team just starting out to tests that a mature team finds hard, and that generic software tests remain necessary on top.

Scoring, from the section "Computing an ML Test Score":

- Half a point if the test is run manually and the result is documented and distributed.
- A full point if a system runs the test automatically and repeatedly.
- Section scores are summed separately.
- The final score is the minimum of the four section scores.

Table V then reads a minimum of 0 as "more of a research project than a productionized system," the interval through 1 as not totally untested but with a real chance of serious holes, the interval through 2 as a first pass at productionization, and the interval from above 3 through 5 as strong enough for mission-critical systems. The authors add that different systems at different points in their life may reasonably aim at different points on that scale. In their survey, none of the 28 tests was implemented by more than 80 percent of the Google teams they looked at. Training-serving skew was among the least implemented and, in their judgment, among the most important.

The minimum is the sentence that matters for this course. A perfect write-up of the model with no data test, no infrastructure test, and no monitoring test scores 0. A memo is not even the half point: the half point requires that the test was executed and the result written down. One automated test in each section is a minimum of 1, which Table V still treats as a system with serious holes. That is an honest description of a teaching case. It is also a reason the FDE claim cannot rest on prose. A course that never runs a data check, a serving check, and a response to a bad signal cannot claim the bottom of this rubric.

Side B therefore says: the twenty decision records, if they are the only blocking work, train a commentator. Principal-level work in this case means a person can reframe the engagement, make one boundary executable, keep one integration honest when the other system does not cooperate, measure something other than a model score, and change a running behavior when the signal is bad. The AWS brief is the infrastructure half of that sentence. Describing a region is not operating, and it is not yet specifying a control a pipeline can reject.

## Where each side overreaches

Side A overreaches when "at most one completion in one file" is applied to the whole credential rather than to a single lesson. Twenty such lessons can all be met, and the ML Test Score minimum can remain 0, because no section ever accumulated an executed test. Point 14 can remain unmet because nothing was monitored and nothing was answered with a runbook action. The dossier is right that a pasted gold file is not judgment. The mirror error is a judgment standing in for a behavior the judgment cannot show. `05-core-versus-extension.md` already hits this on retrieval: a status filter that still scores a staff leaflet is a failed behavior, and fusion is the lab. Side A should keep that split. It should not conclude that the staff-leaflet filter itself may stay a paragraph.

Side A also overreaches if all twenty lesson specifications are blocking locks. Bloom's six-to-one time spread is a school homework figure, not a measure of this cohort, and it still warns that revision time will fan out. Twenty conjunctive locks, each waiting on revision with no tutor, are how a mixed solo cohort stops. The dossier's own assessment note says a large gate repeated twenty times is the failure mode. Making every lesson smaller does not remove the count.

Side B overreaches when "investigate, implement locally, and operate on AWS" is copied onto every lesson. Discovery is forbidden from starting the build. Alpha is forbidden from prototyping the whole journey and from calling throwaway code a live service. Point 14's live-like environment is a property of a service that has reached live, not a property of every learning task. Zinkevich says added complexity slows the next release, and that Phase II waits until the first pipeline is already trusted. The ML Test Score authors say a mission-critical score is not the right aim for every system, and their own Google sample did not clear every test. Requiring a full local platform and a full AWS platform on each of twenty lessons asks a solo learner to outbuild teams who do this as their job. The people who finish will have pasted both stacks. The check will go green. The refusal will not have been studied.

Side B also overreaches if "operate on AWS" means a billed account on the only path. Point 11 asks for cost of ownership and the freedom to change tools later. A card, a free-tier expiry, and a region quota are a timetable and a budget, which this program has already refused as the unit of progress.

## What is carried forward

Keep Phase 1's grain inside a gate: one decision, and at most one new running behavior. Change the count of locks. The twenty traps remain the chapters. They are not twenty doors. Keep the brief's three productions, each at alpha size: a discovery that is allowed to stop, a local behavior that a test can fail, and one cloud control that a local policy can reject. Leave live AWS, and every second subsystem, in a distinction bundle that the capstone may not invent.
