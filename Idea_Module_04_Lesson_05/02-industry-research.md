## Contact-center AI: containment, assist, and the handoff

Three patterns show up across the pages opened. They answer different questions. A lesson that grades only "summary is non-empty" teaches none of them.

**Autonomous containment.** The assistant resolves the contact and the human never sees it. A useful measure is whether the caller's job was finished, not whether a handoff was avoided. Twilio's 22 September 2026 essay (vendor) says containment tells you how often the AI avoided a handoff and does not tell you whether the handoffs that did happen were any good. The measures it names instead are repeat-explanation rate, time to first useful response after transfer, and resolution on escalated conversations compared with conversations that were not escalated. If containment rises and post-handoff resolution stays flat, the assistant is holding work it should pass on. That argument is the right teaching point. The same essay states consumer percentages (context loss, abandonment, a wish for an easy escalation). Those figures are not tied, on the page, to a study this pack opened. **Unverified.** Do not put them in a chapter as facts.

**Agent assist (copilot beside a human).** The human still owns the conversation. The model drafts a note, suggests a next step, or retrieves a policy. McKinsey's April 2024 article "Gen AI in customer care: early successes and challenges" (consulting, not a controlled trial; figures read from the article PDF, which the HTML landing page only links) describes an insurance deployment of an agent-assist tool that sat unused. Agents and managers had not been shown the benefit, and the strongest agents had already memorized the path. Adoption moved after a few early users talked to colleagues. The same article reports a telco copilot with a 40 percent decrease in chat handle time, a 25 percent decrease in costs, and a 14 percent increase in cases resolved per hour, higher for lower-skilled workers. It also describes a narrower summarization add-on: agents had been spending 5 to 10 percent of handle time on after-call notes; the summary cut 10 to 20 seconds per interaction. **Emerging.** Teach the adoption failure and the difference between a note-taking saving and a transformation claim. Do not copy the telco percentages into `rcp/proposal.md` as Al-Waha's target. The article names no dataset a learner can recompute.

**Human handoff with a briefing.** Twilio (vendor) distinguishes a cold transfer (the person starts from a blank screen or a one-line subject) from a warm transfer (the person is briefed before the customer arrives). The design sequence on that page is: write the triggers before the flow (explicit request, high-stakes category, second failure, sentiment shift); write the payload before the code (history, profile, identifiers); summarize before transfer, because a forty-turn transcript gets skimmed and a short summary gets read; keep context when the channel changes; write what the human learned back onto the profile. The summary the essay describes has three beats: what the customer wants, what has been tried, what is still unresolved. That is the twenty-second product. The course's `handoff()` copies a string. It does not ask for those beats, and it does not refuse a summary that invents a clinical urgency.

**Proven** for this course: refuse a blank summary; do not invent an agent or a summary; do not let the assistant assign urgency. **Emerging**: a fixed briefing schema (intent, actions already taken, unresolved, stable identifiers, recommended next step the human may override) and metrics that sit beside containment rather than instead of a safety boundary. **Experimental** for a self-paced lab: a live speech summarizer. The lesson should grade a written briefing against a planted call, not a model demo.

What the forward-deployed engineer owns here is the payload contract, the refusal rules, and the measure of repeat explanation on a scripted set. What the client owns after exit is the contact-center roster that receives the briefing, the phone operation for people who will not use the app, and the decision to expand containment only after post-handoff resolution holds. McKinsey's insurance case is the warning: a well-built assist tool with no peer path is an unused screen.

## Staff unavailability and the clinical calendar

HR leave is not an appointment. The integration pattern is a message from a workforce source that the scheduling system, and only the scheduling system, turns into a change of appointment state.

HL7 FHIR R5 `Appointment.status` is a required code. The code set includes `cancelled`. The definition comment, opened on the R5 appointment definitions page, says that when status is `cancelled`, participants are expected to have their calendars released for that period, and slots marked busy can be set free. Participant records have their own status. Cancelling the appointment is a shared status change with a side effect on the calendar. A lesson that returns `{status: cancelled, event: session.cancelled}` and stops has modeled the signal and skipped the side effect.

Design consequences to teach, as a pattern rather than as an HR product tour:

- The producer is the leave record. The consumer that mutates the session is the scheduling service. A second writer (the assistant, the contact center, the warehouse) will race the calendar.
- Cancel only the sessions of the same practitioner. The course reference already does this. The graded pack never shows a mismatched consultant, so the lesson's paste cannot reveal the bug.
- Time order matters. Leave entered before the session was published is not "late." Late means the roster fact arrived after the session was already offered. Out-of-order delivery and duplicate events are the production cases. Idempotency is the control: the same leave id applied twice yields one cancel.
- Do not cancel a session that has already started or completed. FHIR's status list includes `arrived`, `fulfilled`, `noshow`, and `checked-in`. A leave event that rewrites those is a clinical incident, not a tidy consumer.
- Freeing the slot is part of the cancel, because the comment on `cancelled` says the busy slot can return to free. Patient notification is a separate message. The contact-center handoff of a cancellation is a third message. Collapsing all three into one function hides which human must act.
- Tolerant reading sits on the envelope, not on the clinical rule. Martin Fowler's Tolerant Reader note (9 May 2011) cites Postel's "be conservative in what you do, be liberal in what you accept," and warns that generated bindings break when a provider adds a field that should not have been breaking. The course's unknown-field row (`disposition` on `evt-extra`) is exactly that case. **Proven.** Fowler also says to keep a single reader and to share that reader and its tests with the provider, which is the step toward consumer-driven contracts. **Emerging** for this lesson: a second event version and a test that the old reader still keeps `session.cancelled`. A full schema registry with compatibility modes is **Experimental** as a foundation check. The trace already names it as advanced. It can be a slice on the integration lesson. It should not share the afternoon with the proposal.

The course gate list is not an HL7 specification. Do not teach `session.cancelled` as a FHIR code. Teach it as the cluster's domain event, caused by a leave id, which a scheduling consumer applies under the rules above. If a later lesson touches FHIR, the appointment status to set is `cancelled`, and the slot release is the server's job.

## Benefits, baselines, and the double count

The finance question on `steer-m04-l05` ("Which benefit is counted twice?") is an appraisal question. The paste answers it with "the same calls." The opened guidance says what an evidence pack contains instead.

GOV.UK Service Manual, "Measuring the benefits of your service" (published 20 June 2018): building the wrong thing wastes users' time and pushes them onto less efficient channels. Benefits realisation is a team sport and is usually owned by a product manager or service owner. In discovery, size the problem (time, cost) and establish a baseline, because improvement is only visible against a start point. Assumptions are acceptable early and get tested. Benefits are sorted into cashable (money the organisation can spend, from savings or revenue), non-cashable (a cost avoided later), and wider economic (users' time, or a burden taken off someone outside the organisation). A benefits map runs from drivers through enabling changes and intermediate benefits to the strategic objective. At the end of alpha, an economic model shows the gap between baseline and the estimated improvement, adjusts for optimism bias, and runs sensitivity on the riskiest assumptions (the manual's own example is contact-centre handling time). At beta, agree the metrics with stakeholders before private beta, then compare outturn with the forecast. The decision at the end of beta includes stopping.

"How to set performance metrics for your service" (5 December 2017) adds the measurement chain: a one-line purpose, a few benefits tied to user needs, a hypothesis of the form "doing X will lead to benefit Y because of assumption Z," then a small set of measures that answer "is this service working?" Data sources explicitly include call-centre data, not only web analytics. Context comes from segments (the manual's examples are device and new-versus-returning), from a baseline across all channels, and from a trend rather than a snapshot. Targets are dated. A performance analyst is on the team from discovery. Analysis says what happened, why, and what to do.

The Teal Book, chapter 19 (Government Project Delivery), states the register rule in operational language: each benefit is captured with a category, a measure, and a potential owner; the current performance level is established before work starts and stored on the register as the baseline; after the investment decision, a realisation plan shows the path from baseline to target, the change activities, the assumptions, the dependencies, and the risks. Ownership is handed on. Forecasts that miss are investigated. This pack opened that chapter. It did not treat the book's gateway numbers as the course's G0–G5.

The GOV.UK Digital and Data Benefits framework (to be used with the HM Treasury Green Book, and limited to articulation and monetisation rather than a full tracking method) says teams must keep a benefit counted in one theme from being counted again in another, because digital benefits often show up downstream. The control it names is one team of analysts owning the programme's benefits case from scoping. The course's vendor saving and contact-center saving, both counting the same calls, is this failure in miniature. The framework also notes that capability benefits are easy to double count along a value chain, and that their primary value is enabling other delivery, including AI. A proposal that prices "AI front desk" and "fewer status calls" as two piles of money, when both piles are the status calls, fails this test. The word "same" in a Plan section does not show the reconciliation.

**Proven** for the redesign: a benefits register with baseline, measure, owner, and a single owner of the case so two lines cannot claim one call stream; cashable versus non-cashable versus user-time; a hypothesis; a segment (phone-only at `phc-rim` is already the pack's segment); optimism called out when a target has no series behind it. **Emerging**: a full Green Book economic case inside a rehearsal proposal. The Service Manual points business cases at the Green Book. This course's proposal is practice for a later client, scored inside the architecture component, not a treasury submission. Teach the double-count rule and the baseline. Do not build a five-case model in the same lesson as the bus. **Experimental**: monetised wellbeing or other non-market valuation. Out of scope.

The digit ban on `70` in the metrics check is an authorial leak-prevention rule. Keep seed counts out of chapters. Put the measured series in pack files the learner must cite by id. The checker looks for the citation and for the absence of a second claim on the same calls. It does not look for a magic number, and it does not need to print one.

## Decision memos

Two Amazon forms are in use, and they are not the same document. Mixing them produces a slide narrative with a fashionable name.

The six-page narrative, described by Richard Howard on the AWS Startups Blog (25 September 2019), is a planning document, capped at six pages plus reasonable appendices. It must be readable by someone in the organisation who does not already know the proposal. It opens with purpose (why the document exists, what it will and will not cover) and background grounded in facts. Howard tells the writer to avoid "should" and to show the research. Initiatives are few, with the steps, the expected outcome, and how that outcome was estimated. Risks and dependencies come after the plan, including who else must act. The meeting starts with about fifteen minutes of silent reading, then comments. The document is revised or the team executes. That meeting shape is how a self-paced course can still "hold a steering session": the learner writes the memo, then answers the steer card from memory of the memo, then revises one paragraph the hostile reader broke.

The PR/FAQ, described on workingbackwards.com by the authors of the Working Backwards book, starts from the customer experience and works back to the build. The press release is under a page. The FAQ is five pages or less. The press release names a specific customer, the problem in that customer's words, and a solution that addresses that problem rather than a feature list. The FAQ is the map: external questions a customer would ask, and internal questions about cost and difficulty. The page says the point is the distilled thinking, not a record of all the work. SWOT and a pro forma were tried and did not force the customer question. **Proven** as a product-discovery habit. For this lesson the customer is a phone-only patient at `phc-rim` and a coordinator who receives a handoff, not "the cluster." A PR/FAQ that opens on the sponsor's AI front desk repeats the mistake the course has spent four modules correcting.

Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle is the other named memo habit: the answer first, then the reasons, grouped so a reader who stops at the headings still has the decision. This pack did not open Minto's book. The operational description in hand is Howard's narrative and the PR/FAQ page. Teach answer-first with those. A situation the steering group already accepts (Al-Waha, the drill, the blocked triage problem), a complication (leave arrives late, handoffs arrive empty, two savings count one call stream), the question the complication forces (what is the first build, and what is refused), and the answer (`p-clock`, with identity still in front of the waitlist). **Proven** as a reading order. **Experimental** as a claim that one of these formats predicts better decisions. No opened source in this pack measures that.

The course readout is five short answers. That can be the hostile-reader sheet. It cannot be the proposal. The proposal is the memo they read in the silent fifteen minutes. Appendices hold the register, the handoff schema, and the leave negatives.

## Adoption: what the FDE owns, and what is handed over

Palantir's public Forward Deployed Engineer posting (jobs.lever.co, opened for this pack; a hiring page, so vendor-of-self) describes the role as ownership from the first customer conversation through software that changes how the customer operates, including architecture with other engineers, data that breaks assumptions, custom applications and LLM workflows, and relationships from users to executives. It stresses decisions under incomplete information and work past the current product boundary. A Palantir engineering-blog post on the same distinction returned 403 and is not quoted.

That posting describes the build and the relationship. It does not say the vendor keeps the benefit. The Service Manual does: realisation is usually owned by the product manager or service owner. The Teal Book puts a named benefit owner on the register before work starts and requires the realisation responsibilities to be handed over. McKinsey's insurance assist case shows a tool whose value appeared only after the client's own agents carried it.

Split the capstone's RACI on that line.

The forward-deployed engineer owns, and leaves behind: the payload and the refusal rules; the leave-to-cancel consumer with its negative tests; the metric definitions, including which clock is the wait; the instrumented pilot evidence; the runbook slice for the drill already done; the evidence pack; a named list of risks and of decisions the client has not made. The engineer also owns the truth-telling when the sponsor sentence is the wrong first build.

The client owns, and must be named as owner before G4: clinical safety sign-off (`med-dir` in this course); the benefit, under one analyst or service owner so the two savings cannot both be cashed; the contact center that acts on briefings; the phone channel for the segment that will not use the app; the HR process that either enters leave before publish or accepts that late leave cancels and frees the slot; the on-call rota after the rehearsal team is gone.

GOV.UK "Assisted digital support: an introduction" (updated 18 December 2025) is the analogue for the patient channel, not a law of the fictional cluster. Anyone may need help for lack of trust, confidence, access, skills, or motivation. Support can be phone or in person. The team must research what help people need. A service that claims nobody needs support has to show the research. People using the service as their public-sector job are outside that duty on the UK page. In the course, that split is useful: `phc-rim` phone-only patients are the assisted channel; coordinators and contact-center staff are a change-management audience, closer to McKinsey's agents who already know the steps and will ignore a copilot. One adoption paragraph cannot cover both. **Proven** split. **Emerging**: a peer-champion plan for staff, copied from a single consulting anecdote, worth a short exercise and not a measured claim. **Experimental**: a multi-month ADKAR-style program inside this rehearsal. Out of scope; name the owners and the first week of behavior instead.
