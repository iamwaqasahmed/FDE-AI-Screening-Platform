To: the year drawer, and to Morgan Ibarra because the bay is where the sheets land
From: June Lau, member services
Place: 4th floor, Vancouver
Date: 19 November 2021
Subject: how Harbor Coverage and Cascade Seattle still send the file, and how the telephone is written

I am June Lau. I work member services at the Vancouver desk. I am writing on Friday, 19 November 2021, at the end of an office week that ran Monday through Friday, the same week this desk always keeps. I want the year to say, in my words, what actually arrives in the tray from two organizations, because people have started to talk as if a shared book had already replaced the sheets. It has not. Harbor Coverage still sends files by spreadsheet. Cascade Seattle still sends files by spreadsheet. I open the workbooks. I do not open a log that both of them write. I am not using this page to say what a screen should do with the columns. I am saying what the columns are, as they reach my desk.

Harbor's spreadsheet comes from Ruth Alvarez, or from the mailbox Lena Brooks's office uses when Ruth is not the one who attaches it. Lena is the Harbor Coverage supervisor in Seattle. The workbook has a row for a member matter and a set of columns that do not change their names without a note, though the note is often a sentence in the message rather than a change to the header. I see a reference. I see a region. I see an amount. I see a currency code, and the code is sometimes CAD and sometimes USD, because Harbor is a payer that uses both. I see one or more time cells. I am listing the time cells as cells. I am not circling one of them as the time a board is permitted to show. Chris Pell takes a copy of the amount and the currency into finance. He has told me he does not want me to tidy a currency code before he sees it. I leave the code as the cell has it. A tidy code would be a code I had chosen. I have not chosen.

Cascade's spreadsheet comes from Jordan Hale in records, or from the night tray when Omar Farouk's shift has put a workbook on the wire after I have gone home. The columns are not Harbor's columns. Cascade has its own headers. A referral code may appear in both workbooks in the same week, typed into each sheet by the organization that owns that sheet. A code typed twice is two typings. It is not a table both of them write. I have had Noel Park ask me whether matching codes mean the sheets are one file. I have told him they are two files that mention a string. I am writing that answer here so I do not have to keep it only in the bay. Jordan has not offered me a login to Cascade's book. Ruth has not offered me a login to Harbor's book. They have offered me spreadsheets. I receive what they offer.

The telephone column is digits. I want that sentence to be as dull as the cells. When a phone number arrives from Harbor, it arrives as digits, with no separator that I am supposed to preserve, because there is no separator in the cell. When a phone number arrives from Cascade, it arrives the same way, as digits. A Vancouver number in the sheet is ten digits in a row. A Washington number is ten digits in a row. I will give the shape and not a member's life. A cell that reads 6045550136 is a cell of digits. A cell that reads 2065550148 is a cell of digits. I do not insert a dash after the third digit. I do not insert a dash after the sixth. I do not leave a space where a person might pause in speech. The workbook has already made the number a run of digits, and my copy of the workbook keeps that run. If I spoke the number aloud to a member I would of course breathe. Breathing is not a character in the cell. The stored shape, on these sheets, is digits.

I keep a working sheet of my own at the bay, because member services cannot hold every workbook in its head. My working sheet is a spreadsheet as well. It is not a third authority's book, and it is not a merge I have been authorized to call the record. When I copy a phone into it, I copy the digits as they stand. Noel has a habit, on paper messages, of leaving a gap in a handwritten number. When the source is Harbor's file or Cascade's file, I do not follow Noel's gap. I follow the cell. When the source is a member on the telephone, speaking to me, I still write digits into the working sheet if I am typing into that sheet, because that is the shape the incoming files have trained the column to hold. I am describing a habit of entry. I am not issuing a rule that every notebook in the building must obey, and I am not saying that a later change of shape would be a correction of this one. This year, the files I receive use digits.

Fraser Burnaby does not send me these workbooks. Anita Desai's office is a hospital in British Columbia, on America/Vancouver, and when Fraser needs member services it is usually a telephone call or a short note, not a Cascade-shaped sheet. Priya Shah's name appears on clinical paper that I do not retype into Harbor's columns. Alex Kim's on-call notes stay with the jacket. I am not pulling those notes into the spreadsheet in order to give the spreadsheet a clinical sentence. The spreadsheet is the file Harbor or Cascade sent. It is not a place where I compose a chart.

Cedar Ridge is still a nightly file, not the spreadsheet I am describing. Sam Nguyen sends the night packet, including Saturday and Sunday. Our office is closed those days. On Monday the packet is in the tray, and it is not a Harbor workbook and it is not a Cascade workbook. I do not flatten Sam's packet into Ruth's columns so that the week will look like one grid. Inlet Medical, still its own clinic, sometimes has a small sheet of its own. I do not relabel that sheet as Cedar Ridge. I do not relabel it as Cascade. The name on the sheet is the name I file.

Time cells are the part I most want a later reader to leave alone. Harbor's workbook can carry the time Ruth's office recorded and the time a notice was printed. Cascade's workbook can carry the time Jordan's room wrote and the time a night export was saved. My working sheet can carry the time I typed the row, which is a Vancouver afternoon, America/Vancouver, on a weekday. I have had all of those times in one week, on paper clipped to the message, and I have not picked one of them for a display. Morgan asked me what she should say if a member asks how long a matter has been waiting and the sheets do not agree. I told her I can read her the cells. I cannot hand her a single painted clock from this job, because this job is copying spreadsheets, not awarding a screen. She was not pleased. I am not going to become pleased on her behalf in this note. The cells remain cells.

Wes Calder's page is still in the platform binder. He left last year. I have read the page because it is on the shelf behind the bay, and because members of staff quote it when they are tired of three time cells. The page talks about one instant. The spreadsheets in my tray this November do not arrive already stamped with one instant that I can see. They arrive as workbooks with the times their senders typed. I am not editing his page from member services. I am not saying the spreadsheets have defeated it, and I am not saying the spreadsheets have enacted it. I am saying what I received. Bobbi Tran is in information technology. She is still new enough that I ask her how to open a workbook when a file will not open, and she helps me open it. She does not own the clock page. She has not told me to replace a digit string with a dashed string. Harper Singh is employed at Northline. I am not writing a second employer under that name in order to explain a workbook, because I do not have one. When a Cascade file fails to open, I ask Bobbi. I do not ask a company I do not have.

The rest of the year's ordinary traffic can be summarized without turning it into a system. Reference strings get copied from one sheet to another by hand or by a paste that a person performs. A paste is a paste. It does not create a shared write. Currency codes stay in the currency column. Phone numbers stay digits. The office closes Friday and opens Monday. Saturday's Cedar Ridge packet waits. I will keep typing what the cells contain. I will not, on this page, name the cell a screen is allowed to trust.
