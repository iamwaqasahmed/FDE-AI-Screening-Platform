# What forward deployed engineers actually do

Research date: 30 October 2026. This note records practices that leading organizations describe in public. It does not invent a shared FDE method. Where a page could not be opened, the claim is not treated as proven. Access limits are in [04-sources.md](04-sources.md).

Labels: **Proven** means a page or standard opened for this research. **Emerging** means a company’s own 2025–2026 description of a role that is still being invented. **Speculative** means opinion, an unverified history, or a curriculum inference.

## The role is several jobs that share a name

**Proven, and in conflict.** There is no single job description.

Palantir’s UK careers page, opened in full, still uses the internal split. A Delta, the forward deployed software engineer, “work[s] directly with customers to quickly understand their greatest problems and design and implement breakthrough solutions.” An Echo, the deployment strategist, immerses in “customers’ most intricate workflows,” works with customer teams, and turns a hunch about data into an operational result. A Dev builds the product (Gotham, Foundry) for many customers. Brian, identified on that page as a Delta, is quoted: “We support incredibly sensitive and important work at Palantir, and we must deploy software that works.” That is the opened Palantir record. Longer Palantir blog posts from 2019 through 2025 returned HTTP 403. They are listed in the sources note and are not used as proof.

OpenAI’s own pages, both opened, draw a hard line against professional services. Colin Jarvis, head of forward deployed engineering, wrote on 22 October 2026 that he founded the team in early 2025 to close “the deployment gap”: the distance between what models appear capable of and what people can reliably use. The business FAQ answers “Do FDEs provide professional services?” with “No.” FDEs are customer-facing engineers who work alongside professional-service partners. “If it can already be done with existing AI capabilities, an FDE typically isn’t needed.” DeployCo, described on the same page as an enterprise subsidiary, supplies additional implementation capacity. FDE’s distinctive job is the uncertain path: connect to Product and Research, deliver a measurable operational outcome, then leave the customer able to own the capability.

Anthropic’s live Greenhouse postings, opened, split the same work across two seats. The Applied AI forward deployed engineer (London posting 5423029008) embeds with strategic customers, builds production applications on Claude, ships artifacts such as MCP servers, sub-agents, and agent skills, gives “white glove” deployment support, and codifies patterns back to Product and Engineering. Travel is stated as 25–50%. The Technical Deployment Lead (posting 5017903008) “won’t write production code.” That person owns the engagement from statement of work through production: technical discovery, MVP scope, stakeholder management, value measurement, security reviews, legal approvals, procurement, and “organizational dynamics,” working in highly regulated industries. Travel is again 25–50%.

Ramp’s engineering essay, opened, describes a third shape. FDE at Ramp began in fall 2023 with a very small team supporting the largest customers, then grew to 16 people. The team sits on the whole customer lifecycle: prospect, implementation, and long-tail support. The stated principles are always be scoping, generalize the work, do more, and win when the customer wins. Hiring weights drive and customer empathy above coding perfection, and says AI tools make technical gaps easier to close. Seven of the sixteen were previous founders. This is a company essay about one team, so it is **Emerging**, not a profession-wide standard.

Andreessen Horowitz is **opinion**. Joe Schmidt’s 4 June 2025 essay, “Trading Margin for Moat,” argues that enterprise AI needs a services motion, that FDEs are often professional-services staff “rebranded,” and that founders should trade gross margin for implementation depth. Tom Hollands’s 27 January 2026 essay treats the FDE title as “title arbitrage” and claims Palantir renamed solutions and integration engineers in 2011. That history was not confirmed on any Palantir page this research could open. Both essays were opened and are labeled opinion wherever they are used.

Government delivery manuals describe the seat on the other side of the table. They rarely use the FDE title. They describe what has to be true for a public service to be built and kept. Those manuals are **Proven** as guidance. They are not descriptions of Palantir, OpenAI, or Anthropic.

## What the opened sources agree on

Seven practices show up in more than one opened primary source. They are the core of the job as it is currently described.

**Start from an outcome, then earn the right to build.** OpenAI’s stated team value is “Start with the outcome, do what it takes to succeed, then follow with scale.” An impressive demo is explicitly not the bar; the bar is a system the team can stand behind in production. The business page’s sequence is map processes, define success, prove it in production, scale what works, with stages named Discover, Prototype, Validate, Go live, and Scale. Ramp’s first principle is to question every requirement before accepting a multi-month build. The UK Government Digital Service service manual, discovery guidance last updated 21 June 2021 and still the live manual, says you should not start building in discovery. Discovery exists to learn users, constraints, and policy intent, and to decide whether alpha is worth the cost. Stopping is described as a success if the research says so. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 (AI 100-1, January 2023) says the MAP function should produce enough context for an initial go or no-go on whether to design, develop, or deploy. These sources disagree on timing, which is recorded below. They agree that “the sponsor asked for an assistant” is not yet a scoped piece of work.

**Learn the domain on the engagement.** Jarvis writes that FDEs do not need the industry on day one and that learning it is the job. He describes new hires studying chip design with the customer’s verification engineers, and he wants teams that mix generalists with domain experts. Anthropic lists a vertical background as a plus, not a gate. Ramp treats the ability to de-scope under pressure as a hiring test. **Emerging**, and consistent across the three companies whose pages were opened.

**Connect the system to the work, including the parts with no clean interface.** Schmidt’s opinion essay says agents fail when they are thin wrappers over systems of record, and that the services work is secure connection to databases, APIs, workflows, and business logic. OpenAI’s business page lists workflow automation, an enterprise context layer, process discovery from recordings, and code modernization of legacy systems. Anthropic’s FDE posting says “work within customer systems.” Ramp says enterprise customers have legacy systems and workflows that must be integrated, and that implementation is the bottleneck on scale. None of these pages romanticizes the integration. The work is context, permissions, and the path from a workflow to a reliable action.

**Decide, case by case, whether to customize or to make a reusable capability.** This is the judgment the sources describe, not a slogan. Ramp states the tension inside one team: always-be-scoping pushes toward a small workaround; generalizing pushes toward a feature hundreds of customers can use; “ad-hoc customizations pollute the codebase.” OpenAI’s semiconductor story says custom applications were later rebuilt as plugins and skills on a common harness, removing roughly 90 percent of the custom code, so a second team could be onboarded in a day after a two-month first deployment. The business FAQ says the point of FDE is to raise the floor until the next customer does not need an FDE for that class of problem. Anthropic’s FDE duties include identifying “repeatable deployment patterns” and sending them back to Product and Engineering. **Emerging**, and the default differs by employer. A model lab wants the custom work to disappear into the product. A workflow company wants a platform feature. A government team usually wants a shared component other agencies can reuse, which is the analogue in the US Digital Services Playbook’s “default to open” and the GOV.UK Service Standard’s point on common components. That analogue is an inference and is marked **Speculative** as a curriculum claim in the recommendations note.

**Evaluate against the operational outcome, with the people who do the work.** OpenAI defines success up front as turnaround time, quality, throughput, or manual effort, then tests representative work. A voice deployment sat near 60 percent on the evals that mattered until Research and the field team took it above 90 percent. A tax schedule needed six weeks of engineering and practitioner review to reach 90 percent precision and recall. NIST’s MEASURE function (AI 100-1, section 5.3) requires tests before deployment and in operation, mixed methods, uncertainty, benchmarks, and independent review. Section 1.2.1 says there is no consensus method and that metrics can be gamed or hide groups. Anthropic’s 12 June 2024 essay says there is no standard red-team method, that most of its testing has been in English from a US perspective, and that the useful loop runs from expert probing to a fixed set to model-generated variants. **Proven** as a standard and as those companies’ practice. The missing consensus is part of the finding.

**Own production, then hand it over.** OpenAI’s FAQ says that after launch the FDE sets the customer team up to own the capability. GDS Service Standard point 14, updated 29 January 2026, requires maximum uptime, regular quality assurance that is not left to automated tools, monitoring of user outcomes and ethical issues such as bias, and a plan to respond. The US Digital Services Playbook’s play 12 requires real-time performance monitoring including tail percentiles, automated alerts, a post-mortem path, and a way for the public to report issues. Play 10 requires automated tests and load tests. Play 4 says a minimum viable product should reach users in no more than three months, with releases several times a month. These are **Proven** government practices. They describe operations as a continuing responsibility, not a launch checklist.

**Stay through the organizational friction.** Anthropic’s deployment lead is explicitly accountable for procurement, security review, legal approval, contract changes, and executive briefings. The FDE posting requires balancing competing priorities “with integrity” and conducting discovery with diverse stakeholders. a16z’s Schmidt, in opinion, says being physically present helps “sort through internal power dynamics and drive adoption.” Ramp says FDEs must give an enthusiastic yes to rational requests and push back on unreasonable ones, including in tense conversations about delays and bugs. The US playbook’s play 6 requires one product owner with authority over tasks, features, and technical tradeoffs, and a relationship with the contracting officer. 18F’s de-risking guide, opened, says the same thing as a principle: empower a full-time in-house product owner, and do not treat a stakeholder who knows the program as a substitute for a user. 18F also records that the office’s staff were laid off in 2025; the guide remains a public statement of that practice.

## A working picture, limited to opened sources

An FDE engagement in 2026, using only pages this research opened, runs as follows. Someone who can ship sits with the operator on a hard problem. OpenAI’s public examples are farmer guidance, semiconductor verification, telecom voice, and tax throughput. The team names the outcome in the customer’s units, cuts scope, and builds inside that customer’s systems and permissions. Practitioners help write the evals. If the model cannot do the job, the gap goes to research. If it can, custom glue is replaced when a reusable harness exists. The customer’s staff leave able to run what was built. Beside the builder, someone owns the statement of work, the sponsor, procurement, and the definition of value. Anthropic splits those seats. Ramp holds sales, build, and support in one team. Palantir’s careers page still separates the strategist, the deploying engineer, and the product engineer.

That picture is **Emerging**. It is not a licensed profession, and it is not a Palantir operating manual.

## Conflicts that a curriculum must not flatten

**Professional services versus a product function.** a16z (opinion, June 2025) says the FDE is often a services employee rebranded, and that model labs hired FDEs and solutions engineers to win enterprise deals. OpenAI’s business page (opened) says FDEs are not professional services and are the wrong tool when existing capabilities already suffice. Anthropic runs both a building FDE and a deployment lead who owns the SOW. A course that teaches “FDE means consultant” and a course that teaches “FDE means product engineer on site” are both incomplete. The accurate statement is that employers are currently arguing, in public, about which box the work sits in.

**Customize for one customer versus delete the custom code.** Palantir’s careers page describes Deltas as implementers of breakthrough solutions for the customers in front of them. Ramp prefers the generalized feature and admits the conflict with scoping. OpenAI celebrates removing about 90 percent of custom code once the product can carry the use case. A curriculum that only rewards a bespoke agent, or only rewards a platform abstraction, misses the decision.

**When building is allowed to start.** GDS: do not build the service in discovery; alpha prototypes are for testing the riskiest assumptions and are expected to be thrown away; discovery is often four to eight weeks. US Digital Services Playbook: ship a functioning MVP that solves a core user need in no more than three months, and release often. OpenAI: get something valuable into users’ hands, learn what holds up, then scale; the production standard is higher than a demo. These can be sequenced (learn, prototype, harden). They cannot be taught as the same phase. A self-paced lab that opens with a working pipeline has already chosen the US and OpenAI side of this conflict and skipped the GDS right to stop.

**Who writes code.** Anthropic’s deployment lead is not the coder. Anthropic’s FDE is. Ramp’s FDEs ship core-product features and also negotiate scope. OpenAI’s essay describes FDEs building harnesses, evals, and then retiring them. Government manuals center a product owner who may not be the engineer. The Al-Waha syllabus already rotates lead, AI or data, application, product, and architect. The field sources add a commercial and political owner, and they do not assume every strong contributor codes.

**Presence.** Anthropic states 25–50% travel for both the FDE and the deployment lead. Schmidt (opinion) says physical presence helps with power and adoption. OpenAI’s opened pages do not state a travel percentage. A remote, self-paced lab can teach the artifacts of an engagement. It cannot, by itself, teach the in-person politics those pages describe. That limit should be explicit rather than papered over with more personas.

**Whose history of the title is true.** Hollands (opinion, January 2026) says Palantir coined the title in 2011 by renaming lower-status solutions work. Palantir’s own long account of Echo and Delta could not be opened. The careers page confirms the names Delta, Echo, and Dev, and confirms that Deltas design and implement with customers. It does not confirm the 2011 story. Do not teach the a16z origin tale as Palantir history.

## What this means for this program’s audience

The syllabus trains Saudi government employees who will support AI implementation, then may join a real client project. They are not being hired as OpenAI’s FDE org or as Palantir Deltas. Two translations follow, both **Speculative** as curriculum judgments and grounded in the contrast between the opened sources.

First, graduates will often sit in the government seat the US and UK manuals describe: the product owner, the technical counterpart, the person who must tell a vendor what “done” means, who can stop a use case, and who remains after the vendor leaves. OpenAI’s own handover language says the customer team should own the capability. If the course only rehearses being the vendor’s builder, graduates will be weak on the day a DeployCo or a systems integrator arrives with a statement of work.

Second, the same graduates are asked to do the builder’s work themselves on a real project: discover, architect, implement, integrate, evaluate, operate, present. That is the FDE builder seat Anthropic and OpenAI describe, minus a research lab to escalate model gaps to, and minus a product org that will absorb the custom code. In government the “make it reusable” move is usually a shared platform, a contract clause that the agency owns the code and data, and a team that can run the service. US playbook plays 5 and 13, and 18F’s buying guidance, are the opened sources for that move: frequent deliverables, government control of software and data, a warranty period, and a transition-out plan.

A program that collapses these two seats into “build an agent and present it” will produce people who can demo. The opened sources say the job is the outcome, the constraint, the evaluation with practitioners, the ugly integration, the decision to generalize or to stop, and the handover of a service someone else can operate.

## What a self-paced curriculum has to contain

These practices do not want the same kind of lesson. Some are code. Some are decisions with a legitimate “do not build.” Some are documents a contracting officer would recognize. Operations over time can only be simulated in a lesson.

For mixed-depth government staff, the core is wider than a model lab: an outcome and a right to stop, a whole service, a named owner, a contract that allows iteration and exit, integration that may stay unfinished, evaluation with uncertainty, security outside the prompt, the cost of the running service, operations after launch, and Arabic and accessible use. How the twenty lessons already cover pieces of that list is the next note. The ten capabilities are in [03-recommended-capabilities.md](03-recommended-capabilities.md).
